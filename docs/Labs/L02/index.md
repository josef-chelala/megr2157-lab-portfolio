## Lessons Learned
To start, I have a decent amount of experience with 3D printers since I own two Ender 3D printers at home. Therefore, this entire process and how it works is something I have known for years now. 
<br>
<br>
One thing I learned is that it is possible to use the nozzle itself as a probe for bed leveling. When I used my nozzles at home, I used to go through them quickly when I was trying to learn how to use Z Offsetting to level the bed. Even slightly tapping the bed could partially damage the nozzle. So when I saw that the nozzle was being used to probe, I was in slight disbelief. I could not find an article that clearly explains how this 3D printer model probes, but I found a troubleshooting article that vaguely says that the heatsink for the hothead has a load cell that goes off when the nozzle (I assume) touches the bed ([“Loadcell Troubleshooting”](https://help.prusa3d.com/article/loadcell-troubleshooting_815397)). 
<br>
<br>
Another thing I learned is that Prusa Slicer has an interesting color mixing option for the Core One printer (although I do not believe any in the lab are currently set for that). I noticed it when setting up the basic settings for the Core One printer. Color mixing is basically using multiple filaments of printer colors (cyan, magenta, yellow, and black with white as a additional) to create dozens of different colors. It is done by alternating between the colors for each very thin layer which makes an illusion of different colors from a normal distance away. This does require an addon for the schools printers to work, but it is possible (["Print with Dozens of Colors"](https://blog.prusa3d.com/our-new-open-source-colormix-model-in-prusaslicer-and-easyprint_136079/)).
<br>
<br>
For the next two, I hope that explaining what changes I can make can count towards what I "learned".
<br>
<br>
For one, I would try to figure out how to add ironing to the g-code to give top layers a better finish. Ironing is done by simply moving the tip of the nozzle over the top layer to melt the plastic so each line melts together. This creates a semi-flat looking surface and can even give it a bit of a shine ([The Snapmaker Team](https://www.snapmaker.com/blog/ironing-in-3d-printing/)). 
<br>
<br>
Also, when I normally do larger print jobs, I usually add a brim to the g-code. Usually done only at the first layer or two, a brim is essentially a large outline of the first layer. Its lines are more spread out from one another so that it is easy to take off the bed and so it doesn't get completely attached to the model itself. The first couple layers are extremely important for several reasons. In one way, if the first layer isn't stuck enough to the printer bed, this could lead to edges at the bottom to lift up and off the bed or even lead to serious warping. Worst case, it might just completely come off mid print. The brim reduces the chances of these issues by simply giving the first layer more surface area to work with ([“What Is a Brim"](https://www.creality.com/blog/what-is-a-brim-in-3d-printing)).
